\abstractText{%
Archival photographs usually suffer from several degradations at once:
optical blur, loss of resolution, sensor or film noise and lossy JPEG
compression. Each affects a different part of the image spectrum. This
project studies whether a very small restoration network can exploit that
structure. The proposed network, FCSG-Net, splits the degraded image into
low, mid and high frequency bands with fixed, soft radial masks in the
Fourier domain, processes every band with three shared convolutional experts
of kernel size $7\times7$, $5\times5$ and $3\times3$, and uses a lightweight
gate to select the two most suitable experts for each band at every pixel.
The model has 97,088 parameters and costs 9.39 GFLOPs for a $256\times256$
input. We train FCSG-Net, DnCNN and two FFDNet variants from scratch on the
same synthetic composite degradation of DIV2K images, using one schedule and
one evaluation protocol, after verifying the implementation and reproducing
the published FFDNet result to within 0.01\,dB. On 100 validation images
FCSG-Net reaches 27.24\,dB PSNR, against 27.83\,dB for DnCNN and 28.19\,dB
for blind FFDNet, while using 82.6\% fewer parameters than DnCNN. Its low
FLOP count does not translate into speed: on a Tesla T4 GPU it is the slowest
of the four models. The learned routing is spatially varying and tracks the
noise level, with within-image correlations up to 0.81, and overriding the
gate's selections at inference costs 0.37\,dB on average. Frequency-band
routing thus gives a small model inspectable behaviour, but within this
budget it does not make the model more accurate or faster than simple
convolutional baselines.
}

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Old photographs rarely suffer from a single, clean corruption. A print that
was scanned at modest resolution, stored as a heavily compressed JPEG and
captured in the first place through a soft lens on grainy film carries all of
those defects at once. Each of them leaves its mark on a different part of the
image spectrum. Optical softening removes high spatial frequencies. Resampling
discards detail above a resolution limit and introduces interpolation
artefacts. Sensor and film noise spreads energy roughly evenly across
frequencies, so it dominates wherever the true signal is weak, which in
natural images means the high frequencies. Block-based JPEG compression
quantises the mid and high frequency coefficients of each $8\times8$ block and
leaves blocking and ringing behind.

Most learned restoration networks treat this mixture as one problem. A deep
convolutional network such as DnCNN \cite{zhang2017dncnn} sees the whole
degraded image and learns, implicitly, how to undo every corruption in it.
Larger transformer-based restorers such as Restormer
\cite{zamir2022restormer} are very effective, but their tens of millions of
parameters put them out of reach for the low-cost hardware that an archive,
a library or a hobbyist is likely to own.

This project starts from a simple observation. If different corruptions live
in different frequency bands, it might be useful to split the image into
bands explicitly and let a small network decide, for every band and every
location, which kind of filter suits it best. A large kernel can follow
smooth, slowly varying structure. A small kernel can respond to fine detail
and to the high-frequency residue of noise. A learned gate can choose between
them pixel by pixel. We call the resulting network \fcsg{}, a
frequency-conditioned, spatially gated network, and we study it under a
strict budget: fewer than five million parameters and fewer than ten GFLOPs
for a $256\times256$ input.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

Let $f \in [0,1]^{H\times W\times3}$ be a clean RGB image and let
\begin{equation}
	x = \mathcal{D}(f;\, \theta), \qquad
	\theta = (\sigma_\text{blur},\, s,\, \sigma_n,\, q)
	\label{eq:degradation}
\end{equation}
be a degraded observation produced by Gaussian blur of width
$\sigma_\text{blur}$, bicubic down- and up-sampling by a factor $s$, additive
Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma_n$ and JPEG compression at
quality $q$. The parameters $\theta$ are drawn at random for every image and
are not given to the restoration model. The task is to learn a mapping
$g_\phi$ such that $\hat f = g_\phi(x)$ is close to $f$, measured by PSNR,
SSIM and LPIPS, subject to two hard resource limits:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the network has fewer than $5\times10^{6}$ trainable parameters, and
	\item one forward pass on a $256\times256$ RGB input costs fewer than 10
	reported GFLOPs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Hypothesis and Objectives}\label{sec:objectives}

The working hypothesis of the project is the following. \emph{Spatial top-2
routing over shared convolutional experts of different kernel sizes, applied
after a fixed radial frequency decomposition, yields a routing policy that
varies with image content and with the degradation, and that the trained
network relies on for its output quality, while staying within the stated
budget.} We deliberately phrase this as something to be tested rather than
as a claim of superiority.

The work is organised around four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item[\textbf{O1}] Build \fcsg{} and verify it: exact band reconstruction,
	parameter and FLOP limits, memory, gradient flow and the ability to
	overfit a single image (Chapters~\ref{ch:method} and~\ref{ch:setup}).
	\item[\textbf{O2}] Establish trustworthy baselines by reproducing published
	FFDNet behaviour and training DnCNN on a known Gaussian task
	(Chapter~\ref{ch:setup}).
	\item[\textbf{O3}] Train \fcsg{}, DnCNN and two FFDNet variants under one
	matched composite degradation protocol and compare quality, size, compute
	and measured latency (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
	\item[\textbf{O4}] Characterise the learned routing: where it sends each
	band, how it relates to the sampled degradation, and how much the trained
	model depends on it (Chapter~\ref{ch:routing}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

The contributions of this report are practical and specific:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A frequency-band, spatially routed restoration network with 97,088
	parameters and 9.39 reported GFLOPs at $256\times256$, together with the
	verification tests that establish that it does what its equations say.
	\item A matched comparison in which DnCNN, blind FFDNet, oracle FFDNet and
	\fcsg{} are all trained from scratch on the same synthetic composite
	degradation, with the same schedule, the same number of updates and the
	same evaluation protocol.
	\item A routing analysis that relates per-pixel expert weights to the
	recorded degradation parameters and measures the effect of overriding the
	gate at inference time.
	\item An honest account of the outcome. \fcsg{} reaches 27.24\dB{} against
	27.83\dB{} for DnCNN and 28.19\dB{} for blind FFDNet with 5.7 times fewer
	parameters than DnCNN, but it is not faster on a GPU, because a low FLOP
	count did not translate into low latency.
\end{itemize}
We do not claim state-of-the-art restoration quality, and we do not claim that
frequency decomposition or expert routing are new ideas. Both are well
established in prior work (Chapter~\ref{ch:literature}). What we test is one
particular combination of them under a tight budget.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:literature}

This chapter places \fcsg{} among three lines of work: convolutional
restoration networks, restoration in the frequency domain, and
mixture-of-experts routing. Published scores from these papers come from
different tasks, datasets and noise protocols. We quote them only as context.
None of them appear in our result tables, which contain only models we
trained and evaluated ourselves under one protocol.

\section{Image Restoration with CNNs}\label{sec:lit-cnn}

\paragraph{DnCNN.} Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2017dncnn} showed that a plain
stack of convolutions, batch normalisation and ReLU learns denoising far more
easily when it predicts the noise residual rather than the clean image. The
output is formed by subtracting the predicted residual from the input. Our RGB
implementation has 17 layers of width 64 and 558,403 parameters. DnCNN has no
explicit noise-level input and no notion of frequency bands, which makes it a
clean reference for what a conventional network achieves on our data. The
original paper also studies blind denoising and several restoration tasks, so
it is not limited to one fixed noise level.

\paragraph{FFDNet.} FFDNet \cite{zhang2018ffdnet} takes two ideas further.
It rearranges the input into four half-resolution sub-images before
convolution, which cuts computation by a factor of about four, and it
concatenates a noise-level map to those sub-images so that a single network
can handle a range of noise strengths. The colour model has 12 layers of width
96. The author's KAIR inference release \cite{kair} has 852,108 parameters,
with batch normalisation folded into the convolutions. The paper reports
31.21\dB{} on CBSD68 at $\sigma=25$ using unclipped Gaussian noise. FFDNet
is our second baseline. Its noise map, however, only describes Gaussian noise
and says nothing about blur or JPEG artefacts.

\paragraph{Larger restorers.} NAFNet \cite{chen2022nafnet} shows that
carefully simplified blocks without conventional nonlinear activations can
match far more complex designs, which is a reminder that architectural novelty
alone is weak evidence of progress. Restormer \cite{zamir2022restormer}
represents the heavy end: transposed channel attention and gated feed-forward
blocks with about 26 million parameters in its deblurring configuration. Both
sit outside our budget and serve as context rather than as competitors.

\section{Frequency-Domain Restoration}\label{sec:lit-freq}

\paragraph{MWCNN.} Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2018mwcnn} replace pooling and
upsampling in a U-Net with discrete wavelet and inverse wavelet transforms.
The transforms are fixed and add no parameters, so the convolutional backbone
carries the model's capacity. Importantly, MWCNN processes subbands jointly
and its ablations argue against handling each subband separately. That result
motivates the cross-band fusion stage in \fcsg{}. MWCNN establishes that
explicit frequency decomposition is useful for restoration. It does not route
subbands to different kernel sizes.

\paragraph{Fast Fourier Convolution.} FFC \cite{chi2020ffc} splits channels
between a local convolutional branch and a global branch that operates in the
Fourier domain, which gives every layer an image-wide receptive field. FFC is
an operator, not a single restoration model, and its cost depends on the
backbone it is inserted into. \fcsg{} differs in that it returns each masked
band to the spatial domain before any learned processing.

\paragraph{SFNet and FSNet.} The closest prior work is by Cui et al.
SFNet \cite{cui2023sfnet} uses content-dependent local decomposition with
channel attention to select frequency information, together with a module that
pools globally and in windows to widen the receptive field cheaply. FSNet
\cite{cui2024fsnet} extends the approach to six restoration tasks with
multiple receptive-field scales in one U-shaped network. Both already perform
local, adaptive frequency selection. The distinction we test is narrower:
\fcsg{} uses \emph{fixed} radial masks and puts the learned, spatial decision
in the choice among shared $7\times7$, $5\times5$ and $3\times3$ experts,
which we can then compare with known degradation parameters. Fixed bands on
their own cannot be claimed as an advance over SFNet or FSNet.

\paragraph{FFTformer.} Kong et al.\ \cite{kong2023fftformer} compute
attention-related operations in the frequency domain for deblurring, with
16.6 million parameters in their GoPro model. Their use of local Fourier
processing also shows that the transform's spatial context is part of the
architecture, which is relevant to our tiled inference
(Section~\ref{sec:latency}).

\paragraph{Spectral bias.} Rahaman et al.\ \cite{rahaman2019spectral} show
that ReLU networks tend to fit low-frequency components of a target function
before high-frequency ones. This motivates looking at errors band by band, but
it does not prove that a low band needs a large kernel or that separate
experts will specialise. Those remain empirical questions.

\section{Mixture of Experts and Gating}\label{sec:lit-moe}

Shazeer et al.\ \cite{shazeer2017moe} introduced the sparsely gated
mixture-of-experts layer, in which a noisy top-$k$ gate selects a small subset
of experts for each example and auxiliary importance and load penalties
prevent the gate from collapsing onto a few experts. Those penalties balance
usage across a batch. They are not the same as maximising the routing entropy
of each example, which would push every location towards uniform weights and
work against specialisation. Fedus et al.\ \cite{fedus2022switch} simplify
routing to a single expert per token in the Switch Transformer and discuss
capacity, load balancing and numerical stability at very large scale.

Two points from this literature shape our design and our claims. First, the
\fcsg{} gate is spatial: it produces a routing map over pixels rather than a
single decision per image. Second, the efficiency argument for sparse experts
depends on \emph{conditional computation}, that is, not running the experts
that receive zero weight. Our implementation computes every expert output and
then mixes them with sparse weights. The sparsity is therefore a property of
the weights and of the analysis, not a source of speed, and we account for
the full cost of all experts in every FLOP and latency figure.

The fusion stage uses a squeeze-and-excitation block \cite{hu2018se}, which
pools features globally, compresses them through a small bottleneck and
predicts per-channel scales. In \fcsg{} it reweights the nine band channels
before projecting them back to RGB, at a cost of 96 parameters.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:lit-metrics}

\paragraph{PSNR.} For an image with pixel values in $[0,1]$, the
peak signal-to-noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{PSNR}(\hat f, f) = 10\log_{10}\frac{1}{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat f, f)},
	\qquad
	\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{3HW}\sum_{c,u,v}\big(\hat f_{c,u,v} - f_{c,u,v}\big)^2 .
	\label{eq:psnr}
\end{equation}
We compute PSNR per image over all RGB channels and report the arithmetic mean
over the dataset.

\paragraph{SSIM.} The structural similarity index \cite{wang2004ssim}
compares local means, variances and covariances within a sliding window. We
use an $11\times11$ Gaussian window with $\sigma=1.5$, constants
$C_1=0.01^2$ and $C_2=0.03^2$, and average over channels and positions.

\paragraph{LPIPS.} LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips} measures the distance between
normalised deep features of two images, with channel weights calibrated
against human perceptual judgements. We use the AlexNet variant with inputs
scaled to $[-1,1]$. Lower is better. LPIPS can disagree with PSNR because
perceptual similarity and exact pixel agreement are different properties. The
metric network is an evaluation tool and is not counted in any model's
parameters.

\chapter{Proposed Method: \fcsg}\label{ch:method}

This chapter describes the synthetic degradation used for training and
evaluation, and then the network itself, one module at a time. The notation
follows the implementation in \texttt{models/fcsg.py} and
\texttt{models/blocks.py}.

\section{Degradation Model}\label{sec:degradation}

Archival photographs are degraded in a rough physical order: the lens softens
the image, scanning or resizing loses resolution, the sensor or film adds
noise, and lossy storage compresses whatever is left. Our synthetic pipeline
applies the four operations in that order (Table~\ref{tab:degradation}).
\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Composite degradation pipeline. Parameters are sampled independently for every image.}
	\label{tab:degradation}
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
		\toprule
		Stage & Parameter & Distribution \\
		\midrule
		Gaussian blur        & $\sigma_\text{blur}$ & $\mathcal{U}(0.5,\, 2.0)$ pixels \\
		Down/up resampling   & scale $s$            & $\mathcal{U}(1.0,\, 2.0)$, bicubic both ways \\
		Additive noise       & $\sigma_n$           & $\mathcal{U}(5,\, 50)/255$, clipped to $[0,1]$ \\
		JPEG compression     & quality $q$          & integer, uniform on $\{30,\dots,95\}$, 4:4:4 \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Blur uses Pillow's Gaussian filter. Resampling shrinks the image by $s$ with
bicubic interpolation and enlarges it back to the original size, so input and
output always have the same dimensions. Noise is additive white Gaussian noise
in 8-bit units, clipped to the valid range. JPEG encoding uses Pillow with
chroma subsampling disabled. The image is quantised to 8 bits before the
Pillow blur and before JPEG encoding, as a real file would be.

Each sample carries its four sampled parameters alongside the degraded and
clean images. The network never sees them. They are recorded so that, after
training, we can ask whether the router responds to the degradation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:routing}). With noise between 5 and 50 grey levels and JPEG
quality as low as 30, the mean input PSNR on our validation set is
20.99\dB{}.

\section{Architecture Overview}\label{sec:arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the data flow. The degraded image $x$ is
split into a low, a mid and a high frequency band, each of which is an
ordinary RGB image. Three shared experts with $7\times7$, $5\times5$ and
$3\times3$ kernels process every band. A spatial gate assigns, at each pixel
and for each band, a weight to every expert, keeping only the two largest.
The weighted expert outputs form one restored image per band:
\begin{equation}
	y_b(u,v) = \sum_{e\in\{7,5,3\}} w_{b,e}(u,v)\; E_e(x_b)(u,v),
	\qquad b\in\{L,M,H\}.
	\label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
The three band outputs are concatenated into nine channels, reweighted by a
squeeze-and-excitation block, projected back to RGB and refined. The network
predicts a residual that is added to its input:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{f} = x + \mathrm{Refine}\big(\mathrm{SEFusion}([\,y_L, y_M, y_H\,])\big).
	\label{eq:out}
\end{equation}

	bands; the band dimension is folded into the batch dimension before each

\section{Frequency Decomposition}\label{sec:freqdecomp}

The decomposition is done in the Fourier domain with fixed masks, but the
result of each mask is returned to the spatial domain immediately. We made
this choice early and deliberately. An earlier version of the proposal fed
masked complex spectra straight into the convolutional experts. That has two
problems. A convolution over a spectrum has no clean meaning, since
multiplication in one domain corresponds to convolution in the other, so the
argument that large kernels suit smooth structure no longer applies. And
complex inputs either become two real channels, where phase is discontinuous
and training becomes unstable, or require complex tensor support that many
PyTorch operations lack. Masking in frequency and filtering in space keeps
the decomposition exact and lets every expert be a plain real-valued CNN.

Let $X = \mathcal{F}(x)$ be the two-dimensional real FFT of the image with
orthonormal scaling, and let $r = \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2}$ be the radial
frequency in cycles per pixel. Frequencies are built with \texttt{fftfreq}
and \texttt{rfftfreq} in the unshifted order the FFT uses. Each axis reaches
the Nyquist limit at $0.5$ cycles per pixel. A soft low-pass mask with cutoff
$c$ and transition width $\Delta = 0.05$ is a raised cosine:
\begin{equation}
	t_c(r) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{r-c}{\Delta} + \tfrac12,\ 0,\ 1\right),
	\qquad
	\mathrm{LP}_c(r) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos(\pi\, t_c(r))\big).
	\label{eq:lowpass}
\end{equation}
With cutoffs $r_1 = 0.10$ and $r_2 = 0.25$, the three band masks are
\begin{equation}
	M_L = \mathrm{LP}_{r_1}, \qquad
	M_M = \mathrm{LP}_{r_2} - \mathrm{LP}_{r_1}, \qquad
	M_H = 1 - \mathrm{LP}_{r_2},
	\label{eq:masks}
\end{equation}
and each band image is $x_b = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(M_b \cdot X)$.

Three properties matter. First, the masks sum to one at every frequency, and
the FFT is linear, so $x_L + x_M + x_H = x$ exactly; no information is lost
or invented by the split. We measured a maximum reconstruction error of
$6.0\times10^{-7}$ in single precision. Second, the cutoffs are defined in
cycles per pixel rather than in FFT bin indices. A bin index means a
different physical frequency at $128\times128$ than at full resolution, so
bin-based cutoffs would shift between training crops and test images. With
normalised radii the same sinusoid falls in the same band at both sizes,
which we verified at 128 and 256 pixels. Third, the transitions are smooth.
A hard step in frequency produces ringing in space, and the network would
spend capacity undoing artefacts of its own preprocessing.

The FFT runs in single precision even during mixed-precision training,

\section{Shared Kernel-Size Experts}\label{sec:experts}

There are three experts, $E_7$, $E_5$ and $E_3$, identical except for their
kernel size. Each one processes all three bands, so the parameters are shared
across bands and the number of experts does not grow with the number of
bands.

An expert first applies a lossless $2\times2$ pixel unshuffle
\cite{shi2016espcn}, turning a three-channel image into twelve channels at
half resolution, as FFDNet does. A $1\times1$ convolution expands these to 48
channels, followed by ReLU. Four residual blocks follow. Each block contains
two depthwise-separable stages \cite{chollet2017xception}: a $k\times k$
depthwise convolution and a $1\times1$ pointwise convolution, with a ReLU
after the first stage. A $1\times1$ tail maps back to twelve channels and is
added to the unshuffled input, and a pixel shuffle restores the original
resolution. Odd image sizes are padded by replication and cropped back.

Separable convolutions and half-resolution processing are what make the
budget possible. The experts run nine times per image, once per band for each
of three experts, and dense full-resolution convolutions at width 48 would
exceed ten GFLOPs by a wide margin.

We refer to the experts only by kernel size. Labels such as ``texture
expert'' or ``edge expert'' would be claims about what the experts learned,
and we have no attribution experiment that supports them.

\section{Spatial Top-2 Gate}\label{sec:gate}

A routing decision that is made once per image would amount to nine numbers
computed from a handful of global statistics. That is closer to a lookup
table than to a router, and it is likely to settle on a near-constant choice.
The \fcsg{} gate instead decides per location, so that a smooth sky and a
textured roof in the same photograph can be treated differently.

The gate stacks the three RGB bands into nine channels and computes, in
non-overlapping $16\times16$ windows, the local mean and the local mean of
squares of each channel. These eighteen statistics pass through a
two-layer $1\times1$ network ($18\to24\to9$, ReLU in between) that outputs
nine logits $a_{b,e}$, one per band and expert. The logits are divided by a
temperature $\tau = 1$ and bilinearly upsampled to full resolution. At each
pixel and for each band the two largest logits are kept and normalised with a
softmax; the third expert gets zero weight:
\begin{equation}
	w_{b,e}(u,v) =
	\begin{cases}
		\dfrac{\exp a_{b,e}(u,v)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_b(u,v)} \exp a_{b,j}(u,v)}
		& e \in \mathcal{T}_b(u,v),\\[2ex]
		0 & \text{otherwise},
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:topk}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_b(u,v)$ is the set of the two highest-scoring experts.
The gate also returns the dense softmax over all three experts, $p_{b,e}$,
which is used by the entropy term of the loss. The whole gate has 681
parameters. Figure~\ref{fig:gate} shows the expert and gate operations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{expert_gate.pdf}
	\caption{Expert and gate operations. Experts use pixel rearrangement and
	depthwise-separable residual blocks; the gate pools band statistics in
	$16\times16$ windows.}
	\label{fig:gate}
\end{figure}

As noted in Section~\ref{sec:lit-moe}, every expert is evaluated before
mixing. Expert outputs at locations with zero weight are computed and then
multiplied by zero. Turning the sparse weights into saved computation would
require tiled conditional dispatch, which we did not implement.

\section{Fusion and Residual Refinement}\label{sec:fusion}

The three weighted band outputs are concatenated into nine channels. A
squeeze-and-excitation block \cite{hu2018se} pools them globally, passes them
through a $9\to3\to9$ bottleneck with a sigmoid, and scales each channel. A
$1\times1$ convolution projects the result to three channels. Three
$3\times3$ convolutions ($3\to16\to16\to3$, ReLU between them) refine it, and
the output is added to the degraded input as in Equation~\eqref{eq:out}.
Predicting a residual rather than the image itself is the central lesson of
DnCNN \cite{zhang2017dncnn}, and it lets the network start from the identity.

\section{Training Objective}\label{sec:loss}

The loss has three terms:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\text{char}
	+ 0.05\,\mathcal{L}_\text{freq}
	+ \lambda_\text{ent}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_\text{ent}.
	\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

The Charbonnier loss \cite{charbonnier1994} is a smooth variant of L1,
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_\text{char} = \operatorname{mean}\sqrt{(\hat f - f)^2 + \varepsilon^2},
	\qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-3},
\end{equation}
which behaves like L2 near zero and like L1 for large errors.

The frequency term is the mean absolute value of the orthonormal FFT of the
prediction error, $\mathcal{L}_\text{freq} = \operatorname{mean}\lvert
\mathcal{F}(\hat f - f)\rvert$. It weights errors at every frequency equally,
which counteracts the tendency of pixel losses to favour the dominant low
frequencies.

The entropy term is the negative dense routing entropy, summed over bands and
averaged over the batch and pixels:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_\text{ent} = \sum_b \operatorname{mean}_{n,u,v}\sum_e p_{b,e}\log p_{b,e}.
\end{equation}
Minimising it maximises entropy, which keeps all three experts in use early
in training so that none of them is starved of gradient. A constant entropy
bonus would, however, push the router towards uniform weights forever, which
is the opposite of what we want to observe. The coefficient therefore decays
linearly, $\lambda_\text{ent}(t) = 0.01\,(1 - t/T)$, reaching zero at the
last update $T$. Because the sign of this term is easy to flip by accident,
the verification suite checks that it decreases as the weights approach
uniform. DnCNN and FFDNet are trained with the same Charbonnier and
frequency terms and no entropy term.

\chapter{Experimental Setup and Verification}\label{ch:setup}

The numbers in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are only as good as the code and
protocol behind them. This chapter describes the data, the baselines and how
every model was trained and evaluated, and then the tests we ran before
committing GPU time to long training runs.

\section{Dataset and Data Pipeline}\label{sec:data}

All experiments use DIV2K \cite{agustsson2017div2k}, which provides 800
high-resolution training images and 100 validation images. Training uses only
the training split; the 100 validation images are used for every result we
report. Because we did not hold out a separate test set, the reported numbers
are validation results, a limitation we return to in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

Our first data loader decoded a full-resolution PNG for every crop, and
training ran at roughly 1.8 steps per second because the GPU waited on the
CPU. We replaced it with a cache of 32 fixed $128\times128$ crops from each
training image, 25,600 tiles in all, stored as a single 1.26\,GB array of
8-bit pixels and memory-mapped at training time. Random flips and
90-degree rotations augment the cached crops. The degradation of
Section~\ref{sec:degradation} is still applied on the fly by four loader
workers, so every epoch sees freshly sampled blur, scale, noise and JPEG
settings. In effect the set of degraded pairs is unlimited, while the set of
clean crops is fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pipeline.pdf}
	\caption{Training data pipeline. Loader workers sample degradation
	parameters for each augmented clean crop and return the degraded input, the
	clean target and the parameters.}
	\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Online degradation is good for training but awkward for anyone trying to
reproduce exact inputs. We therefore also exported a fixed set of 5,000
degraded and clean pairs generated with seed 1234, together with a manifest of
their parameters. The degradation code reproduces this set byte for byte, and
a single CPU worker generates about 233 samples per second.

\section{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

\paragraph{DnCNN.} Our RGB DnCNN has 17 convolutional layers of width 64
with batch normalisation and predicts the residual, giving 558,403
parameters.

\paragraph{FFDNet.} Our FFDNet follows the author's colour architecture
\cite{kair}: pixel unshuffle, 12 convolutional layers of width 96 and a
noise-level map concatenated to the input, giving 852,108 parameters. On our
composite task the noise map is a problem, because there is more to the
degradation than Gaussian noise. We therefore train two variants.
\emph{Oracle} FFDNet receives the true sampled noise level $\sigma_n$ as its
map. This is information no real user would have, so the oracle variant is an
upper reference rather than a fair competitor. \emph{Blind} FFDNet receives
a constant map of $27.5/255$, the middle of the noise range, during both
training and evaluation. It sees exactly what DnCNN and \fcsg{} see.

All four models are trained from scratch on the same composite degradation.
We do not compare against numbers published for other tasks or datasets.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evalproto}

Final results use all 100 DIV2K validation images. Each image is degraded
once with a seed equal to its index, so every model sees exactly the same
degraded inputs. Inference is tiled: $256\times256$ tiles with 32 pixels of
overlap (stride 224), with the last tile in each direction aligned to the
image border and overlapping outputs averaged uniformly. Tiling keeps memory
low on large images; Section~\ref{sec:latency} measures how much it costs in
accuracy. Restored values are clipped to $[0,1]$ before computing PSNR, SSIM
and LPIPS as described in Section~\ref{sec:lit-metrics}.

During training we monitored a different quantity: PSNR on 16 fixed
$128\times128$ validation crops. The two are not comparable. For \fcsg{} the
final crop PSNR is 30.59\dB{} while the full-image result is 27.24\dB{}.
Crop numbers are used only for convergence curves.

The wall-clock panel exposes a cost that the update count hides. \fcsg{}
needed 10.06 hours for 200,000 updates, against 6.66 for DnCNN and about two
for FFDNet. A separate throughput test, excluding warm-up, validation and
checkpointing, measured 5.84 steps per second for \fcsg{}, 9.41 for DnCNN
and 26.67 for FFDNet. Matching the number of updates therefore did not match
the GPU time, and \fcsg{} received the most of it.

\section{Latency, Memory and Tiling}\label{sec:latency}

\paragraph{Latency.} The motivation for a small model is that it should be
cheap to run. Table~\ref{tab:latency} shows that, on the GPU we used, this
did not hold. Measurements are median wall-clock times for one FP32 forward
pass at batch size 1 on a Tesla T4, with TF32 disabled, after 20 warm-up
passes and over 50 timed repetitions.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Median forward latency (ms) by input size and peak inference memory at $256\times256$ (Tesla T4, FP32, batch 1).}
	\label{tab:latency}
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
		\toprule
		Method & $128^2$ & $256^2$ & $512^2$ & Peak MB at $256^2$ \\
		\midrule
		\fcsg{}          & 5.10 & 19.40 & 75.59 & 51.3 \\
		DnCNN            & 4.22 & 18.04 & 72.47 & 37.8 \\
		FFDNet, blind    & 1.69 &  6.30 & 24.12 & 20.2 \\
		FFDNet, oracle   & 1.69 &  6.35 & 24.62 & 20.2 \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\fcsg{} is the slowest model at every size, slightly behind DnCNN and three
times slower than FFDNet, despite needing an eighth of DnCNN's reported FLOPs
and a third of FFDNet's. It also uses the most memory. We did not profile
individual operators, so the following explanation is our best reading of the
architecture rather than a measurement. Each forward pass runs nine expert
passes, all of them in full, because zero-weighted experts are not skipped.
Depthwise convolutions perform very little arithmetic per byte they read, so
on a GPU their running time is governed by memory traffic, not by the FLOP
count. The network is also made of many small layers, so kernel launch
overhead is a large fraction of the total, and the FFT and inverse FFT add
work that the FLOP estimate counts only approximately. FFDNet, by contrast,
runs one stack of dense $3\times3$ convolutions at half resolution, which is
exactly the kind of workload GPUs are built for.

The practical lesson is that the FLOP budget we set at the start of the
project was the wrong proxy for speed on this hardware. Figure~\ref{fig:cost}
plots quality against both measures. Against FLOPs, \fcsg{} sits far to the
left of the other models. Against latency it sits to the right of FFDNet with
lower quality, so on a T4 it is dominated. Whether the picture changes on a
CPU or a mobile accelerator, where depthwise convolutions are better suited,
remains to be tested.

\chapter{Routing Analysis}\label{ch:routing}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} asked how well \fcsg{} restores images. This chapter
asks what its gate actually does. All measurements here use the final
200,000-update checkpoint from Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. They were taken on
ten fixed validation images (0801, 0812, \dots, 0900), each degraded with
eight different random seeds, giving 80 samples. For each sample we take the
$256\times256$ centre crop after degrading the whole image. The ten source
images are the independent units; the eight seeds per image vary only the
degradation.

\section{Training Dynamics of the Gate}\label{sec:routing-training}

The entropy term in the loss is meant to keep all three experts in use early
on and then fade out so that the gate can commit. Figure~\ref{fig:entropy}
shows the dense routing entropy during training, summed over the three bands.
Its maximum possible value is $3\ln 3 = 3.296$ nats, reached when every
expert gets equal probability everywhere.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{training_entropy.pdf}
	\caption{Dense routing entropy summed over bands during training. The
	maximum is $3\ln 3 \approx 3.296$ nats.}
	\label{fig:entropy}
\end{figure}

At the end of training the dense probabilities remain close to uniform. On
the diagnostic samples the mean dense entropy per band is 1.089, 1.096 and
1.097 nats for the low, mid and high bands, against a maximum of
$\ln 3 = 1.099$. The sparse top-2 weights are similarly close to an even
split, at 0.684, 0.691 and 0.692 nats against $\ln 2 = 0.693$. Taken alone,
these numbers would suggest that the gate learned almost nothing. The rest of
this chapter shows why that conclusion would be wrong: small differences
between logits still change \emph{which} two experts are selected, and the
selection turns out to matter.

\section{Spatial Routing Statistics}\label{sec:routing-stats}

Figure~\ref{fig:routing-summary} summarises the routing over the 80 samples.
Because two experts are selected at each pixel, the selection fractions in
each band add up to two.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{routing_summary.pdf}
	\caption{Mean sparse weight and selection fraction for each expert and band
	over 80 samples.}
	\label{fig:routing-summary}
\end{figure}

The clearest pattern is in the high band, where the $3\times3$ expert is
selected at 99.98\% of pixels. The other slot is shared between the larger
experts, which still carry substantial weight on average (about 0.26 for the
$7\times7$ expert and 0.22 for the $5\times5$). The high band is therefore
not handed entirely to the small kernel; rather, the small kernel is always
present and one of the larger kernels is mixed in depending on the location.
This agrees with the intuition behind the design, that fine-scale content is
best handled with a small support, though the routing data alone cannot show
that the $3\times3$ expert is doing anything specific to high frequencies.

Figure~\ref{fig:routing-map} shows the routing maps for one image. The maps
follow image structure: selection changes along object boundaries and between
smooth and textured regions. Because a top-2 choice switches abruptly when
two logits exchange rank, smooth underlying probabilities can still produce
sharp boundaries between selected pairs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{routing_0834.pdf}
	\caption{Expert weights for validation image 0834 (seed 33). Rows: low, mid
	and high band. Columns: degraded crop and the weights of the $7\times7$,
	$5\times5$ and $3\times3$ experts, on a common 0 to 1 scale.}
	\label{fig:routing-map}
\end{figure}

These maps describe the gate's output. They do not establish what each
expert contributes to a particular restored structure, which would require
attribution experiments we did not run.

\section{Routing Versus Degradation Parameters}\label{sec:routing-corr}

Because every sample records its degradation parameters, we can ask whether
the routing responds to them. For each image we subtract its eight-sample
mean from both the mean routing weights and the degradation parameters, so
that the comparison is within images and not driven by differences in image
content. We then compute Pearson correlations between the nine mean expert
weights and the four parameters.

Several of these correlations are strong. The mean weight of the $3\times3$
expert in the high band correlates at $r = +0.81$ with the noise level: the
noisier the input, the more the gate leans on the small kernel for high
frequencies. In the low band, the $5\times5$ expert's weight correlates at
$+0.80$ with noise and the $7\times7$ expert's at $-0.73$, so with more noise
the gate shifts low-band weight from the largest kernel to the medium one.
The mid band follows the same direction, with the $3\times3$ weight at
$+0.77$. The six strongest correlations are all with the noise level. The
strongest with blur is $+0.42$ and with JPEG quality $-0.39$, so within this
sample the gate responds mainly to noise.
Figure~\ref{fig:corr} shows the full correlation matrix next to the
intervention results of the next section.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{correlations_interventions.pdf}
	\caption{Left: within-image Pearson correlations between mean routing
	weights and degradation parameters. Right: per-image PSNR change under the
	inference interventions of Table~\ref{tab:interventions}.}
	\label{fig:corr}
\end{figure}

We treat these results as exploratory. There are 36 correlations computed on
ten source images, without correction for multiple comparisons and without
image-level confidence intervals. The four parameters also vary together in
every sample, so a correlation with noise cannot be fully separated from the
other corruptions. A controlled sweep that varies one parameter at a time
would be needed to make a causal statement. What the data do show is that
the gate's output is not constant: it moves with the degradation in a
consistent direction across images.

\section{Inference-Time Interventions}\label{sec:interventions}

Correlation shows that the gate responds to its input. It does not show that
the network needs that response. To test this we kept the trained weights
fixed and replaced the gate's output at inference time in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Equal pair weights}: keep the two experts the gate selects at
	each pixel, but give each a weight of one half.
	\item \emph{Uniform weights}: ignore the gate and give all three experts a
	weight of one third everywhere.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:interventions} reports the change in PSNR on the ten images.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{PSNR change when the trained gate is overridden at inference (10 images).}
	\label{tab:interventions}
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
		\toprule
		Intervention & Mean $\Delta$PSNR & Min / max \\
		\midrule
		Equal weights on the selected pair    & $-0.040$\dB & $-0.137$ / $+0.028$ \\
		Uniform weights over all three experts & $-0.372$\dB & $-0.986$ / $+0.163$ \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The two interventions separate two parts of the gate's decision. Flattening
the weights within the selected pair costs only 0.04\dB{} on average, so the
exact mixing proportions matter little. Removing the selection itself costs
0.37\dB{} on average and up to almost 1\dB{} on one image. The trained
network therefore depends mainly on \emph{which} experts are combined at each
pixel, and much less on how they are weighted. This also explains how the
gate can matter while its entropy stays near the maximum: a near-uniform
distribution over three experts still leaves one of them consistently
excluded.

A limitation of this test is that it measures dependence on the gate of
\emph{this} trained network. Its experts and fusion stage were trained
alongside the gate and adapted to its choices, so overriding the gate puts
the model in a situation it never saw. The result shows that the gate is
doing real work in the trained model. It does not show that a model trained
from the start without a gate would be worse. Answering that requires a
matched training ablation, which we list as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Putting Chapters~\ref{ch:results} and~\ref{ch:routing} together gives a
mixed but coherent picture. The routing mechanism works in the sense we set
out to test. The gate produces spatially varying maps, its choices track the
noise level in a consistent direction, and the trained model loses up to a
decibel when its choices are overridden. These are the behaviours the
design was meant to produce.

What the routing does not do is make the model competitive. With fewer than
a hundred thousand parameters, \fcsg{} ends up 0.6 to 1.0\dB{} behind
baselines that are six to nine times larger, and on a GPU it is not faster
than any of them. The likely reason for the quality gap is simply capacity.
Even the largest expert has under 40,000 parameters, and much of the
network's width is spent on processing three bands that sum to the original
image. Blind FFDNet, the strongest fair model, gets its advantage from a
plain and dense architecture, not from extra information.

A useful way to read the result is that frequency-band routing is a cheap way
to make a small model's behaviour \emph{interpretable}: we can see where
each kernel size is used and relate it to the degradation. Within the budget
we chose, it is not by itself a way to make the model better or faster.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary of Findings}\label{sec:summary}

We set out to test whether a very small restoration network could split an
image into frequency bands and route each band, pixel by pixel, to
convolutional experts of different kernel sizes, and whether the learned
routing would be meaningful. We return to the four objectives of
Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

\paragraph{O1, build and verify.} \fcsg{} has 97,088 parameters and costs
9.39 reported GFLOPs at $256\times256$, well inside the limits of five million
and ten. It passed every verification check before training: exact band
reconstruction, consistent frequency units across image sizes, gradient flow,
mixed-precision execution, single-image overfitting and exact checkpoint
restoration (Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

\paragraph{O2, trustworthy baselines.} Our FFDNet implementation reproduces
the published CBSD68 result to within 0.01\dB{} using the author's weights,
and our DnCNN training loop reaches 32.65\dB{} on DIV2K for Gaussian
denoising (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-repro}).

\paragraph{O3, matched comparison.} Under one composite degradation and one
training protocol, \fcsg{} reaches 27.24\dB{} PSNR, 0.753 SSIM and 0.396
LPIPS on 100 DIV2K validation images. This is 0.59\dB{} below DnCNN and
0.95\dB{} below blind FFDNet, and it loses to FFDNet on every one of the 100
images. It does so with 82.6\% fewer parameters than DnCNN and 88.6\% fewer
than FFDNet. Its low FLOP count, however, did not make it fast: on a Tesla T4
it is the slowest of the four models (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

\paragraph{O4, the routing.} The learned gate varies across the image,
selects the $3\times3$ expert almost everywhere in the high band, and shifts
its weights with the noise level, with within-image correlations up to
$r=+0.81$. Overriding its selections at inference costs 0.37\dB{} on average,
while flattening its weights within the selected pair costs only 0.04\dB{},
so the trained model depends on which experts are chosen more than on how
they are mixed (Chapter~\ref{ch:routing}).

In short, frequency-band routing produced a small model whose behaviour can
be inspected and related to the degradation, but within our budget it did
not produce a model that is better or faster than simple convolutional
baselines.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound how far these results can be taken.

Each model was trained once with an unfixed seed, so we have no estimate of
run-to-run variance. The consistency of the gap across images makes it
unlikely to be an accident of one seed, but small differences, such as
between the two FFDNet variants, cannot be interpreted. All results are on
the DIV2K validation split, which was also used to monitor training; there is
no untouched test set.

The degradation is synthetic. Real archival photographs suffer from fading,
scratches, dust, colour casts and scanner artefacts that our pipeline does
not model, and we did not evaluate on real archival images or ask people to
judge the results. The good performance of any of these models on our data
says nothing yet about real archives.

The routing analysis uses ten source images. Its correlations are
exploratory, the four degradation parameters vary together, and there is no
correction for the number of comparisons. The inference interventions
measure the trained model's dependence on its gate, not the value of having a
gate during training.

The cost measurements cover one GPU, the Tesla T4, at batch size 1 in FP32.
We did not profile individual operators, so our explanation of why \fcsg{} is
slow is a reasoned account rather than a measurement. We did not measure
latency on CPUs or mobile hardware, nor peak training memory at batch 16.

Finally, \fcsg{} computes all nine expert passes regardless of the gate, so
its sparse routing does not save computation.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future}

The most important next step is a matched training ablation: train \fcsg{}
and an otherwise identical model with fixed uniform weights from the same
initialisation and seed, and compare them on the full validation set,
preferably with several seeds each. This is the only experiment that can
show whether learning a router helps, rather than whether a trained model
depends on its router. Related ablations would replace the three kernel sizes
with three identical $3\times3$ experts, replace the spatial gate with a
single per-image gate, replace SE fusion with plain concatenation, and
replace the soft masks with hard ones. Each of these isolates one design
decision of Chapter~\ref{ch:method}.

The second direction is to turn sparse weights into sparse computation. Since
the trained model mostly cares about which two experts are chosen, skipping
the third expert in tiles where it is never selected could reduce the
running cost by up to a third. This should be preceded by an operator-level
profile, because if latency is dominated by memory traffic and launch
overhead, removing arithmetic may not help much. Measuring latency on a CPU
and on a mobile accelerator would show whether the model's low FLOP count
is worth more on hardware that suits depthwise convolutions.

Third, the capacity gap could be addressed directly by widening the experts
within the parameter budget, which is about fifty times larger than what the
model uses. The compute budget is nearly exhausted, so this would have to go
together with cheaper expert evaluation, for example by sharing one
unshuffled feature map across bands. Letting the gate see a larger context than a $16\times16$
window may also help.

Finally, any claim about archival restoration needs real archival
photographs, a controlled degradation sweep to separate the effect of each
corruption on the routing, and a held-out test set that was never used
during development.
