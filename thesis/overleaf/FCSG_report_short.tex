\abstractText{%
Archival photographs usually suffer from several degradations at once:
optical blur, loss of resolution, sensor or film noise and lossy JPEG
compression. Each affects a different part of the image spectrum. This
project studies whether a very small restoration network can exploit that
structure. The proposed network, FCSG-Net, splits the degraded image into
low, mid and high frequency bands with fixed, soft radial masks in the
Fourier domain, processes every band with three shared convolutional experts
of kernel size $7\times7$, $5\times5$ and $3\times3$, and uses a lightweight
gate to select the two most suitable experts for each band at every pixel.
The model has 97,088 parameters and costs 9.39 GFLOPs for a $256\times256$
input. We train FCSG-Net, DnCNN and two FFDNet variants from scratch on the
same synthetic composite degradation of DIV2K images, using one schedule and
one evaluation protocol, after verifying the implementation and reproducing
the published FFDNet result to within 0.01\,dB. On 100 validation images
FCSG-Net reaches 27.24\,dB PSNR, against 27.83\,dB for DnCNN and 28.19\,dB
for blind FFDNet, while using 82.6\% fewer parameters than DnCNN. Its low
FLOP count does not translate into speed: on a Tesla T4 GPU it is the slowest
of the four models. The learned routing is spatially varying and tracks the
noise level, with within-image correlations up to 0.81, and overriding the
gate's selections at inference costs 0.37\,dB on average. Frequency-band
routing thus gives a small model inspectable behaviour, but within this
budget it does not make the model more accurate or faster than simple
convolutional baselines.
}

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation and Problem}\label{sec:motivation}

Old photographs rarely suffer from a single corruption. A print scanned at
modest resolution, stored as a compressed JPEG and taken through a soft lens
on grainy film carries all of those defects at once, and each affects a
different part of the spectrum. Blur removes high frequencies, resampling
discards detail above a resolution limit, noise dominates wherever the signal
is weak (in natural images, the high frequencies), and JPEG quantises the mid
and high frequencies of each $8\times8$ block. Most restoration networks
treat the mixture as one problem, and the strongest of them, such as
Restormer \cite{zamir2022restormer}, need tens of millions of parameters.

This project asks whether a very small network can use that spectral
structure explicitly: split the image into frequency bands, and let a learned
gate choose, for every band and every pixel, between convolutional experts of
different kernel sizes. We call the network \fcsg{}.

Formally, a clean RGB image $f\in[0,1]^{H\times W\times 3}$ is degraded as
$x=\mathcal{D}(f;\theta)$ with $\theta=(\sigma_\text{blur}, s, \sigma_n, q)$:
Gaussian blur, bicubic down- and up-sampling by $s$, Gaussian noise of
standard deviation $\sigma_n$, and JPEG at quality $q$. The parameters are
random per image and hidden from the model. We seek $\hat f=g_\phi(x)$ close
to $f$ in PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, with fewer than five million parameters and
fewer than ten GFLOPs for a $256\times256$ input.

\section{Objectives and Contributions}\label{sec:objectives}

We test the hypothesis that \emph{spatial top-2 routing over shared
kernel-size experts, applied after a fixed frequency decomposition, yields a
routing policy that varies with content and degradation and that the trained
network relies on, within the budget.} The objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item[\textbf{O1}] build \fcsg{} and verify it before training;
	\item[\textbf{O2}] establish trustworthy baselines by reproducing a
	published FFDNet result;
	\item[\textbf{O3}] compare \fcsg{}, DnCNN and two FFDNet variants trained
	under one matched protocol, in quality, size and measured speed;
	\item[\textbf{O4}] characterise the learned routing.
\end{enumerate}

The contributions are a 97,088-parameter network with its verification
suite, a matched composite-degradation benchmark, a routing analysis linking
expert weights to the recorded degradation, and a plain account of the
outcome: \fcsg{} is within 0.6\dB{} of DnCNN with 5.7 times fewer parameters,
but it is not faster on a GPU. We make no state-of-the-art or novelty claim;
frequency decomposition and expert routing are both established
(Chapter~\ref{ch:literature}).

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews related work, Chapter~\ref{ch:method}
describes the method, Chapter~\ref{ch:setup} the experimental setup and
verification, Chapter~\ref{ch:results} the results and routing analysis, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes.

\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:literature}

Published scores from the works below use other tasks, datasets or noise
protocols, so they serve only as context. Our result tables contain only
models we trained and evaluated ourselves.

\paragraph{CNN restoration.} DnCNN \cite{zhang2017dncnn} showed that a deep
stack of convolutions with batch normalisation learns denoising much more
easily by predicting the noise residual. FFDNet \cite{zhang2018ffdnet}
processes four half-resolution sub-images and takes a noise-level map as an
extra input, so one network covers a range of noise strengths; the author's
colour model \cite{kair} has 852,108 parameters and reports 31.21\dB{} on
CBSD68 at $\sigma=25$. Its map describes only Gaussian noise, not blur or
JPEG. NAFNet \cite{chen2022nafnet} and Restormer \cite{zamir2022restormer}
mark the efficient and heavy ends of modern restoration and lie outside our
budget.

\paragraph{Frequency-domain restoration.} MWCNN \cite{liu2018mwcnn} replaces
pooling in a U-Net with wavelet transforms and processes subbands jointly,
which motivates our cross-band fusion. Fast Fourier Convolution
\cite{chi2020ffc} adds a global Fourier branch to each layer. FFTformer
\cite{kong2023fftformer} computes attention-related operations in the
frequency domain. The closest work is SFNet \cite{cui2023sfnet} and FSNet
\cite{cui2024fsnet}, which already perform local, content-adaptive frequency
selection. Our distinction is narrower: \fcsg{} uses \emph{fixed} radial
masks and puts the learned spatial decision in the choice among shared
$7\times7$, $5\times5$ and $3\times3$ experts, which we can then compare with
known degradation parameters.

\paragraph{Mixture of experts.} Shazeer et al.\ \cite{shazeer2017moe} route
each example to a few experts with a top-$k$ gate and balance load with
auxiliary penalties; Switch Transformers \cite{fedus2022switch} simplify this
to one expert per token. The efficiency argument for sparse experts depends on
not running the unselected ones. Our implementation computes every expert
and mixes them with sparse weights, so sparsity here aids analysis, not
speed. The fusion stage uses squeeze-and-excitation \cite{hu2018se}.

\paragraph{Metrics.} PSNR is $10\log_{10}(1/\mathrm{MSE})$ on $[0,1]$ RGB
images, computed per image and averaged. SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim} uses an
$11\times11$ Gaussian window ($\sigma=1.5$). LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips}
measures distance between deep features (AlexNet variant); lower is better,
and it can disagree with PSNR.

Table~\ref{tab:gap} summarises the positioning. Each ingredient of \fcsg{} is
established; the project studies one combination of them under a tight
budget.

	\label{tab:gap}
	\small
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccl@{}}

		\midrule

\chapter{Proposed Method: \fcsg}\label{ch:method}

\section{Degradation Model}\label{sec:degradation}

The synthetic pipeline applies four operations in physical order
(Table~\ref{tab:degradation}). The image is quantised to 8 bits before blur
and before JPEG encoding. Each sample stores its sampled parameters, which the
network never sees but which we use to analyse the routing in
Section~\ref{sec:routing}. The mean input PSNR on our validation set is
20.99\dB{}.

		\midrule

	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

The input is split into low, mid and high frequency bands, each an ordinary
RGB image. Three shared experts $E_7,E_5,E_3$ process every band, and a
spatial gate gives each expert a weight per band and pixel, keeping the top
two (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}):
\begin{align}
	y_b(u,v) &= \textstyle\sum_{e} w_{b,e}(u,v)\, E_e(x_b)(u,v), \qquad b\in\{L,M,H\},\label{eq:mix}\\
	\hat f &= x + \mathrm{Refine}\big(\mathrm{SEFusion}([y_L,y_M,y_H])\big).\label{eq:out}
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{architecture.pdf}
	\caption{\fcsg{} data flow. Expert parameters are shared across bands.}
	\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Frequency decomposition.} With $X$ the orthonormal real FFT of
$x$ and $r$ the radial frequency in cycles per pixel, a soft low-pass mask of
cutoff $c$ and width $\Delta=0.05$ is
$\mathrm{LP}_c(r)=\tfrac12\big(1+\cos(\pi\,\mathrm{clip}((r-c)/\Delta+\tfrac12,0,1))\big)$.
With $r_1=0.10$ and $r_2=0.25$ the band masks are $M_L=\mathrm{LP}_{r_1}$,
$M_M=\mathrm{LP}_{r_2}-\mathrm{LP}_{r_1}$ and $M_H=1-\mathrm{LP}_{r_2}$, and
$x_b=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(M_b X)$. Three choices matter. The masks sum to one, so
$x_L+x_M+x_H=x$ exactly. Cutoffs are normalised frequencies rather than FFT
bin indices, so a band means the same thing on a $128\times128$ training crop
as on a full image. And each band returns to the spatial domain before any
learning: a convolution over a spectrum has no clear meaning and complex
inputs make training unstable, so the experts stay plain real-valued CNNs.

\paragraph{Experts.} Each expert applies a $2\times2$ pixel unshuffle
\cite{shi2016espcn} (12 channels at half resolution), a $1\times1$
convolution to 48 channels, four residual blocks of two depthwise-separable
stages \cite{chollet2017xception} with kernel $k\in\{7,5,3\}$, a $1\times1$
tail added to the unshuffled input, and a pixel shuffle back. Separable,
half-resolution processing is what keeps nine expert passes inside the
compute budget. We name the experts only by kernel size, since we have no
evidence about what each one specialises in.

\paragraph{Spatial top-2 gate.} A per-image gate would be a lookup table
that tends to settle on a constant choice. Our gate pools the mean and mean of
squares of the nine band channels in $16\times16$ windows, maps these 18
statistics through a $18\to24\to9$ network to one logit per band and expert,
and upsamples the logits to full resolution. At each pixel the two largest
logits per band are kept and softmax-normalised; the third expert gets zero
weight. The dense softmax $p_{b,e}$ over all three is kept for the loss. The
gate has 681 parameters. All experts are still computed before mixing.

\paragraph{Fusion and refinement.} The nine band channels pass through a
squeeze-and-excitation block \cite{hu2018se}, a $1\times1$ projection to RGB
and three $3\times3$ convolutions ($3\to16\to16\to3$). The output is a
residual added to the input, as in DnCNN.

\section{Training Objective}\label{sec:loss}

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_\text{char}+0.05\,\mathcal{L}_\text{freq}+\lambda_\text{ent}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_\text{ent}.
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{L}_\text{char}=\operatorname{mean}\sqrt{(\hat f-f)^2+\varepsilon^2}$,
$\varepsilon=10^{-3}$, is the Charbonnier loss \cite{charbonnier1994}.
$\mathcal{L}_\text{freq}$ is the mean absolute FFT of the error, which weights
all frequencies equally. $\mathcal{L}_\text{ent}=\sum_b\operatorname{mean}
\sum_e p_{b,e}\log p_{b,e}$ is negative routing entropy; minimising it keeps
all experts in use early on. Its coefficient decays linearly from 0.01 to 0
so that the gate is free to commit by the end. The baselines use the same
loss without the entropy term.

\section{Size and Compute}\label{sec:size}

\fcsg{} has 97,088 parameters: 39,228, 30,012 and 23,868 in the $7\times7$,
$5\times5$ and $3\times3$ experts, 681 in the gate, 96 in fusion and 3,203 in
refinement. We count each multiply-accumulate as two FLOPs and add an
estimate for the FFTs; a $256\times256$ pass costs 4.66 GMACs, or 9.39
GFLOPs, inside the limit. Section~\ref{sec:cost} shows this count was a poor
predictor of running time.

\chapter{Experimental Setup}\label{ch:setup}

\section{Data, Baselines and Training}\label{sec:training}

We use DIV2K \cite{agustsson2017div2k}: 800 training images and 100
validation images, the latter used for every reported result. Training uses
a cache of 32 fixed $128\times128$ crops per training image (25,600 tiles),
with random flips and rotations, and applies the degradation on the fly, so
every epoch sees new corruptions. A fixed set of 5,000 pairs (seed 1234) was
also exported for reproducibility.

The baselines are an RGB DnCNN (17 layers, width 64, 558,403 parameters) and
FFDNet in the author's colour configuration (852,108 parameters) in two
variants. \emph{Oracle} FFDNet receives the true sampled noise level, which
no real user would have, so it is an upper reference. \emph{Blind} FFDNet
receives a constant map of $27.5/255$ and sees exactly what the other models
see. All four models are trained from scratch with the same settings
(Table~\ref{tab:hparams}). Each model was trained once with an unfixed seed,
on a Tesla T4 in Kaggle sessions that resume from checkpoints.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evalproto}

Each of the 100 validation images is degraded once with a seed equal to its
index, so all models see identical inputs. Inference uses $256\times256$
tiles with 32-pixel overlap, averaged where tiles overlap. Training-time
validation on 16 fixed crops is used only for monitoring; it is not
comparable with full-image results (30.59 against 27.24\dB{} for \fcsg{}).

\section{Verification and Baseline Reproduction}\label{sec:verification}

Before training, a notebook on the same T4 ran the checks in
Table~\ref{tab:gates} and stopped at any failure. The overfit check is the
most useful: a model that cannot memorise one image in 200 steps has a bug,
and it is better to learn that in seconds than after ten hours of training.

\section{Computational Cost}\label{sec:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:latency} reports median FP32 forward latency at batch 1 on a
Tesla T4 after warm-up.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Median forward latency (ms) and peak inference memory at $256^2$ (Tesla T4, FP32).}
	\label{tab:latency}
	\small
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
		\toprule
		Method & $128^2$ & $256^2$ & $512^2$ & Peak MB \\
		\midrule
		\fcsg{}          & 5.10 & 19.40 & 75.59 & 51.3 \\

Despite needing an eighth of DnCNN's FLOPs and a third of FFDNet's, \fcsg{} is
the slowest model at every size and uses the most memory. Training showed the
same pattern: 5.84 steps per second against 9.41 for DnCNN and 26.67 for
FFDNet. We did not profile individual operators, so the explanation is our
reading of the architecture: nine full expert passes per image, depthwise
convolutions whose speed is limited by memory traffic rather than arithmetic,
many small layers with launch overhead, and the FFTs. FFDNet runs one stack
of dense $3\times3$ convolutions, which GPUs execute very efficiently. The FLOP
budget set at the start was therefore the wrong proxy for speed on this
hardware (Figure~\ref{fig:cost}). Tiling is not the cause of the quality gap:
changing the tile size from 256 to 512 pixels changes \fcsg{} by only
0.013\dB{} on a ten-image subset.

\section{Routing Analysis}\label{sec:routing}

We analysed the final \fcsg{} checkpoint on ten validation images, each
degraded with eight seeds (80 samples, ten independent images).

\paragraph{Where the gate routes.} The dense routing probabilities end close
to uniform (mean entropy 1.09 to 1.10 nats per band against a maximum of
1.10), which alone would suggest the gate learned nothing. The top-2
selection says otherwise. In the high band the $3\times3$ expert is selected
at 99.98\% of pixels, with the second slot shared between the larger experts
depending on location. The maps follow image structure
(Figure~\ref{fig:routing-map}), and because top-2 selection switches when two
logits swap rank, nearly uniform probabilities can still give a consistent
and sharp choice.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{routing_0834.pdf}
	\caption{Expert weights for validation image 0834. Rows: low, mid, high
	band. Columns: degraded crop and weights of the $7\times7$, $5\times5$ and
	$3\times3$ experts.}
	\label{fig:routing-map}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Routing versus degradation.} After removing each image's mean,
the high-band $3\times3$ weight correlates at $r=+0.81$ with the noise level,
the low-band $5\times5$ weight at $+0.80$ and the low-band $7\times7$ weight
at $-0.73$: with more noise, the gate moves weight towards smaller kernels.
The six strongest correlations are all with noise; the strongest with blur
is $+0.42$ and with JPEG quality $-0.39$. These are exploratory results from
ten images and 36 correlations without multiple-comparison correction, and
the four parameters vary together, so they show a consistent response but
not a causal one.

\paragraph{Does the model need the gate?} Keeping the trained weights and
overriding the gate at inference gives the changes in
Table~\ref{tab:interventions}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{PSNR change when the trained gate is overridden at inference (10 images).}
	\label{tab:interventions}
	\small
	\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
		\toprule
		Intervention & Mean $\Delta$PSNR & Min / max \\
		\midrule
		Equal weights on the selected pair     & $-0.040$\dB & $-0.137$ / $+0.028$ \\
		Uniform weights over all three experts & $-0.372$\dB & $-0.986$ / $+0.163$ \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Flattening the weights within the chosen pair barely matters, while removing
the choice itself costs 0.37\dB{} on average and nearly 1\dB{} on one image.
The trained network depends on \emph{which} experts are combined far more than
on how they are weighted. This shows the gate does real work in this model;
whether a model trained without a gate would be worse needs a matched training
ablation, which we leave to future work.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The routing works in the sense we set out to test: it varies spatially,
tracks the noise level and matters to the trained model. It does not make the
model competitive. With under 100,000 parameters, \fcsg{} trails baselines six
to nine times larger by 0.6 to 1.0\dB{}, most likely for lack of capacity, and
on a GPU it is not faster. Frequency-band routing is a cheap way to make a
small model's behaviour inspectable; within our budget it is not by itself a
way to make it better or faster.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:summary}

\fcsg{} splits an image into three frequency bands and routes each band, per
pixel, to two of three shared convolutional experts. With 97,088 parameters
and 9.39 GFLOPs it passed every pre-training check (O1), and our FFDNet code
reproduced the published CBSD68 result to within 0.01\dB{} (O2). Under one
matched composite-degradation protocol it reached 27.24\dB{}, 0.59\dB{} below
DnCNN and 0.95\dB{} below blind FFDNet, with 82.6\% and 88.6\% fewer
parameters respectively, yet it was the slowest model on a Tesla T4 (O3).
Its gate varies spatially, tracks the noise level with correlations up to
0.81, and the trained model loses 0.37\dB{} when its selections are
overridden (O4). Frequency-band routing gave a small model inspectable
behaviour, but not better accuracy or speed than simple baselines.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Each model was trained once, and all results are on the validation split
with no untouched test set. The degradation is synthetic; we did not test on
real archival photographs or run a human study. The routing analysis uses ten
images and exploratory correlations, and the interventions measure the
trained model's dependence on its gate rather than the value of having one.
Latency was measured on one GPU without an operator profile. All experts are
always computed, so sparse routing saves no computation.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future}

The most important next step is a matched training ablation comparing
learned routing against fixed uniform weights from the same initialisation,
with several seeds, followed by ablations of the kernel sizes, a per-image
gate, plain concatenation instead of SE fusion, and hard masks. Since the
model relies mainly on which experts it picks, skipping unselected experts
could cut up to a third of the expert cost, but an operator profile should
come first, along with latency measurements on CPU and mobile hardware.
Widening the experts within the parameter budget, which is fifty times larger
than the model uses, would address the capacity gap if cheaper expert
evaluation frees compute. Finally, any claim about archival restoration
needs real archival images, a controlled degradation sweep and a held-out
test set.
